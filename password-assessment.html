Password Security Assessment

Testing credential strength on a deliberately vulnerable Linux training target, and turning the result into a defensive case.

Carried out in my isolated lab against Metasploitable2, an intentionally vulnerable image built for security training. No real systems were involved, and this write-up focuses on method and defensive lessons rather than operational detail.

Goal

Credential strength is one of the most common weak points on any network. The goal here was to assess how well a system's passwords would hold up, and to understand why weak or reused passwords are such a serious risk — so I can recommend better practices with evidence behind them.

Approach

Working from my Parrot box against the training target on the isolated segment, I:

  • Confirmed connectivity between the two machines.
  • Scanned the target to inventory its running services and identify where it was exposed.
  • Identified a known-vulnerable, unpatched service as the weak point — the kind of finding a vulnerability scan would flag as critical.
  • Assessed the strength of the system's stored password hashes using John the Ripper, a standard password-auditing tool.

What the assessment showed

The results made the risk concrete. Of the seven accounts with usable passwords, six were recovered almost instantly — every one of them was either the account name itself or a trivial, guessable string. Only a single account with a password outside common wordlists held out.

In other words, once an attacker reaches the stored hashes, weak passwords offer essentially no protection. The standard auditing tools recover them in seconds.

Why it mattered — the defensive case

  • Patch known-vulnerable services. The whole chain started with one unpatched service. Keeping software current closes that first door.
  • Enforce strong, unique passwords. Weak or reused credentials are what turn a single foothold into full compromise. Length and unpredictability are what make hashes expensive to crack.
  • Defense in depth. No single control is enough — patching, password policy, and least privilege each remove a step an attacker depends on.

Takeaway: this is why password policy isn't bureaucratic box-ticking. Seeing how fast weak credentials fall is the most convincing argument there is for strong ones.
